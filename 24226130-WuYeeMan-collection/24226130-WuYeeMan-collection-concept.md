How Close Is Close Enough?

This collection started from something my granny told me. She explained that a copied key is never exactly the same as the original. Small differences can appear in the teeth and shape. If a copy is used to make another copy, these differences may continue to build up. Each version can move a little further away from the original.

I wanted to compare one original key with its copy. They can perform the same function, but they are not completely the same. I recorded them through images, sounds, text, touch, smell and measurement. All twenty records look at the same question from different ways: how much of the original remains in a copy?

The title How Close Is Close Enough? asks when we decide that a copy is “the same” as the original. The differences can be very small, but they still exist. For me, the loss is not something disappearing suddenly. It happens through copying, use, change and time.

The keys also made me think about repetition in everyday life. Things can seem the same while small changes continue to build up over time.

I placed my granny’s Taishanese recording near the end of the collection, followed by an English translation. Her words were the starting point of this work, so I wanted them to become its ending too. I also wanted to preserve her voice as a memory between us.

This collection cannot hold everything. Photographs cannot fully show the smallest details. Text cannot reproduce touch or smell. Sound cannot record the physical resistance of turning a key. The Taishanese recording keeps my granny’s voice, but some people may not understand it. The translation keeps the meaning, but loses her accent, rhythm and tone.

In this sense, the collection itself becomes another key: a key that may one day open this memory again.
